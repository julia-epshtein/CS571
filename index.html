1 Racial Inequality in Sentencing

The Three Strikes law aimed to target repeat offenders, but it has worsened racial disparities in sentencing. Black and Latino defendants face harsher penalties due to biases in arrests and prosecutions.

In counties that enforce the law aggressively, people often receive life sentences for minor offenses, reinforcing deep inequalities in the justice system.

% Minority by County

The bar charts show striking racial disparities in California’s prisons. Despite being smaller portions of the state’s population, Black and Latino individuals make up over 70% of inmates. Black people account for 28% of prisoners but only 6% of California’s population. Similarly, Latinos make up 46% of prisoners while being just 38% of residents.

Black individuals also face the highest imprisonment rate—over 1,600 per 100,000 residents—far exceeding rates for other racial groups. Clearly, prison demographics don’t match California’s population, but instead expose systemic biases that disproportionately affect communities of color.

Second vs. Third Strikes

This chart shows how sentence lengths vary between second and third strikers.

Among Second Strikers, about 38.8% receive sentences longer than 50 months. While that’s already significant, the contrast becomes stark with Third Strikers, nearly 70% of whom fall into the 50+ months category — often indicating life sentences or decades-long incarceration.

This sharp jump underscores how the Three Strikes Law severely escalates punishment at the third offense, frequently resulting in extremely long sentences regardless of offense severity. The chart visually reinforces the law’s disproportional impact on repeat offenders, many of whom may be incarcerated for nonviolent or lower-level crimes.

County-Level Imprisonment Trends

These heatmaps show imprisonment rates and costs across all California counties from 2009-2016. Darker colors indicate higher values.

On the left, we observe strikingly high incarceration rates in certain counties over time: Kings County, consistently the highest, peaking at 1568.5 per 100,000 Tulare, Tehama, Shasta, and Yuba Counties, all with rates above 1000 per 100,000 in at least one year These counties are predominantly rural, raising questions about how location, economic status, and judicial culture shape incarceration outcomes Meanwhile, counties like San Francisco and Marin maintain relatively low rates, pointing to significant regional disparities in sentencing patterns.

The right heatmap illustrates the financial cost of imprisonment across California counties from 2009 to 2011, measured in millions of dollars. Notably, Los Angeles County far surpasses all others, spending over $2.6 billion per year—a reflection of both its large incarcerated population and its size. Other high-cost counties include Orange, San Bernardino, Santa Clara, and Riverside, each consistently exceeding $500 million annually. In contrast, smaller or rural counties such as Trinity, Modoc, and Alpine report costs under $5 million, despite some having high incarceration rates. This disparity highlights how imprisonment costs are influenced not only by sentencing practices, but also by population size and jail infrastructure, and it underscores the enormous economic burden incarceration places on the state.

Poverty and Imprisonment Relationship

This scatterplot shows the relationship between poverty rates and imprisonment rates across California counties. Each point represents a county, with the regression line showing the overall trend. Use the controls to explore different years.

Across all years, there is a clear positive correlation: counties with higher poverty rates tend to report higher incarceration rates. This pattern underscores how economic hardship may be linked to increased criminalization, whether through over-policing in low-income areas, lack of access to legal defense, or systemic cycles of poverty and incarceration. Users can scroll through the years to observe how consistent this correlation remains, with some counties persistently in the upper-right quadrant—signaling both high poverty and high imprisonment.

Imprisonment Trends Over Time

These charts show the historical trends of imprisonment rates and costs across all California counties from 2009-2016.

These line charts reveal contrasting trends in incarceration rates and associated costs across California counties from 2009 to 2016. On the left, the red line shows a steady decline in imprisonment rates, dropping from about 660 per 100,000 residents in 2009 to under 500 by 2016— a likely result of reform efforts such as Proposition 36 and Proposition 47, which reduced sentences for non-violent offenders.

However, the blue line on the right shows that imprisonment costs have risen, climbing from approximately $138 million to over $160 million despite fewer people being incarcerated. This suggests that the cost of incarceration per individual has increased, potentially due to rising healthcare expenses, an aging prison population, or increased facility operating costs. Together, these charts highlight a critical mismatch: while reforms may reduce the number of prisoners, systemic inefficiencies continue to drive up the financial burden.
